Design thinking: six steps to keep repeating

Design thinking is a human-centred, iterative approach. The aim is to understand the right problem before investing heavily in a solution.

  1. Empathize. Observe and engage with real people. Learn about their context, constraints, motivations, and needs.
  2. Define. Turn what you learned into a clear problem statement. A “How might we…” question can help focus the team.
  3. Ideate. Generate a range of possible solutions before choosing a direction.
  4. Prototype. Build a simple, low-cost version—such as a sketch, mock-up, or role-play—to make the idea tangible.
  5. Test. Put the prototype in front of users. Observe, listen, and gather feedback without defending the idea.
  6. Iterate. Use what you learned to improve the solution, revisit the problem, or try a new prototype.

The notes also discussed the Business Model Canvas as a way to map nine connected areas: customer segments, value proposition, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key activities, key resources, key partners, and cost structure.

Understand the whole market

A platform may serve more than one group. A service marketplace, for example, has both providers and customers. Teams need to understand what each side values and make the exchange useful for both; participation on one side can also affect the value offered to the other.

The workshop notes grouped common motivations to explore during user research:

  • Time: getting a task done quickly or helping a provider spend less time on administration.
  • Money: finding better value, increasing income, or reducing costs.
  • Lifestyle: supporting a preferred way of living or working.
  • Emotions: helping people feel safe, confident, appreciated, or proud.
  • Relationships: creating connection, community, or lasting customer relationships.
  • Experience: making a service intuitive, reliable, and pleasant to use.

These are prompts for investigation, not assumptions about every user. The team should check which motivations matter through observation and testing.

Funding a startup: the trade-offs

The session compared several ways a startup might raise money and the costs each can bring:

  • Bootstrapping: using savings, revenue, or side work. It can preserve ownership and encourage disciplined spending.
  • Friends and family: early support based on personal relationships, which can put those relationships under pressure if the idea is not yet proven.
  • Grants: funding that may not require repayment or equity, but often comes with applications and reporting requirements.
  • Debt: a loan that must be repaid with interest, creating regular obligations.
  • Equity: investment in exchange for ownership, which can reduce a founder’s share of future profits and control.

The right option depends on the business, its stage, and the team’s goals. Founders should understand the terms before committing.

Put the workshop into practice

  • Use the six-step cycle to validate a problem you care about.
  • If your idea is a platform, define the needs of each side of the market.
  • Research funding options and learn how debt and equity affect a venture.
  • Keep iterating after the pitch: listen to users, test, and improve.

The project doesn’t end at the pitch—keep iterating.
